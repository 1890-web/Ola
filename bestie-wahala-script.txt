If trouble ever needed a best friend, it would choose Ugochi. And within one week, Ugochi would start a fight with trouble.

If you stepped on Nneka's foot at the market, Ugochi would step on yours twice. If you looked at Nneka for too long, Ugochi would ask you what you were looking at. And if you said one wrong word to Nneka, Ugochi would tie her wrapper tighter, roll her shoulders, and say, "Say it again. I am waiting."

Whenever anybody asked why she was always fighting, Ugochi would hiss and say, "What is a friend for? If you touch Nneka, you touch me. And if you touch me, you have touched fire."

Nneka and Ugochi had been best friends since primary school in Umuoji. Nneka was quiet, gentle and soft-spoken. She ran a small provisions shop by the junction, selling sugar, milk, matches and biscuits. Ugochi was the opposite. Loud, dramatic and fashionable, she sold hair attachments in the market and could argue with a whole bus park without losing her voice.

Everywhere Ugochi went, wahala followed her like a loyal dog.

In church, she quarrelled with the choir mistress because Nneka was not given a solo. Nneka had never asked for a solo. At the market, she fought a tomato seller who refused to add one extra tomato for Nneka. Nneka had not even wanted the tomato. And at Nneka's own shop, Ugochi once chased away a customer who was bargaining too much. "Oya, carry your money and go," she told the poor woman. "This is a provisions shop, not a charity." That customer never came back. Neither did her sister, her neighbour, or her neighbour's sister.

The people of Umuoji had learned to adjust. Market women started serving Nneka first, just to avoid the commotion. The choir mistress gave Nneka two lines in every special number, just to keep the peace. And whenever anybody saw Ugochi walking towards them with her hands on her waist, they quietly remembered somewhere else they had to be.

But here is the strange part. Ugochi was not a wicked woman. No, no, no. She was the most loyal friend anybody could ask for. When Nneka's father died, Ugochi closed her own stall for a whole month and slept on Nneka's floor so that she would not cry alone. When Nneka fell sick with malaria, it was Ugochi who carried her on her back to the clinic in the rain. And whenever Nneka was sad, Ugochi was the one who made her laugh until her stomach hurt.

So Nneka never complained. She simply apologised to everyone that Ugochi offended. In fact, Nneka said sorry so often that the children in the compound nicknamed her Sister Sorry.

Then one evening, Nneka came to Ugochi's stall with news that made Ugochi scream so loudly the whole market turned to look.

Kelechi wanted to marry her.

Kelechi was a quiet schoolteacher who bought biscuits from Nneka's shop every morning, even on days he wasn't hungry. Everybody in the junction already knew he was in love. Everybody except Nneka.

"Ugochi," Nneka said, holding her friend's hands. "His family is coming for the introduction next Saturday. Please, I am begging you. Whatever happens that day, don't fight anybody. Just smile."

Ugochi placed a hand over her heart. "Me? Fight? Nneka, you don't know me at all."

Nneka knew her very well. That was exactly the problem.

That same week, Nneka's Aunty Ifeyinwa came from Onitsha to help with the preparations. On Thursday evening, she gathered everybody in the parlour. "Listen carefully," she said. "I know Kelechi's family. His mother is a very traditional woman. In that family, on the day of the introduction, the groom's mother will insult the bride's people on purpose. She will complain about everything. It is an old custom. A test. If the bride's family stays calm, she respects them forever. If they fight, she leaves."

Everybody nodded seriously.

Everybody except Ugochi, who had stepped outside at that exact moment to buy suya from the man by the gate. By the time she came back, licking pepper from her fingers, she only heard Aunty's last words. "So whatever she says, let her talk."

"Let her talk?" Ugochi repeated. "Of course. Let her talk. And then we will see."

Remember that suya. It is important.

On the morning of the introduction, Ugochi arrived two hours early. She had appointed herself head of protocol. She wore a stunning sleeveless orange gown with a wide scoop neckline, gold earrings as big as bangles, and a gele so tall the gate had to be opened fully for her to enter. She inspected the chairs. She tasted the jollof. She told the drinks boy to arrange the malt from tallest to shortest. Then she stood in front of the mirror and practised her smile.

But Ugochi's smile looked like somebody preparing for war.

Aunty Ifeyinwa noticed. She walked over and whispered, "Ugochi, remember. Whatever she says, let her talk."

"Don't worry, Aunty," Ugochi whispered back, cracking her knuckles one by one. "I will let her talk. I will let her talk very well."

Aunty looked at those knuckles and decided to pray quietly.

At exactly twelve o'clock, three cars stopped in front of the compound. Kelechi's uncles came out first, carrying cartons of drinks and a big bag of kola nuts. Then came a goat, tied to a rope and looking very unhappy about the whole event. And finally, stepping out of the last car like a queen arriving at her palace, came Kelechi's mother. Mama Kelechi.

She was a tall woman with a sharp face, a stiff lace wrapper, and eyes that had clearly never been impressed by anything.

The moment she sat down, she began.

"Is this the compound? It is smaller than I expected."

Ugochi's left eye twitched. Nneka squeezed her hand under the table.

Mama Kelechi tasted the jollof rice. "Too much pepper. Who cooked this? Did they want to kill us?"

Ugochi had cooked the jollof. She bit her lip so hard that it changed colour. Aunty Ifeyinwa smiled calmly and said nothing.

Then Mama Kelechi looked at the drinks. "Malt? Only malt? In our time, the bride's family brought palm wine from three different villages."

Ugochi picked up a kola nut and started chewing it very, very fast. She was counting under her breath. One. Two. Three. Four. You know that kind of counting a mother does when a child is about to receive the slap of his life? Yes. That was the counting Ugochi was doing. She reached twenty-seven before Mama Kelechi spoke again.

"And this bride," Mama Kelechi continued, looking Nneka up and down. "She is too thin. My son needs a strong woman who can carry water, not a broomstick."

That was it.

Ugochi rose from her chair so slowly that it was frightening. She adjusted her gele, rolled her shoulders, and said in the calmest voice anybody had ever heard from her, "Madam. Who is the broomstick?"

Nneka grabbed her arm. "Ugochi, please—"

But it was too late. A rocket does not return to the ground once it has been lit.

"You came to our house," Ugochi said, her voice rising, "you drank our malt, you ate our jollof, and now you are insulting the bride? Ah, no. Not in this compound. Not while I am alive!"

What followed was a scene the people of Umuoji would talk about for many years.

Ugochi began carrying back the drinks. Carton by carton, she marched them out of the compound and loaded them into the groom's car. "Take your drinks! We have our own malt!" Then she picked up the bag of kola nuts and handed it to the nearest uncle. "Take your kola! We are not hungry!" When the uncles tried to calm her down, she picked up the broom from the corner of the compound and waved it in the air like a sword.

The uncles ran. Kelechi's cousins ran. Even the goat, seeing the broom, broke its rope and ran out of the gate, through the junction and straight into Nneka's provisions shop, where it knocked over a whole shelf of sugar.

And Mama Kelechi? She stood up slowly, gathered her lace, looked at Ugochi for a long moment, and walked to her car without a single word.

The cars drove off. The music stopped. And in the middle of the compound, still holding the broom, stood Ugochi, breathing heavily, her magnificent gele leaning to one side like a tired tree.

Nneka sat down on the floor and cried.

Ugochi dropped the broom. "Nneka… I was only defending you."

But Nneka could not even answer.

Just when it seemed like the marriage was finished before it even started, Kelechi's car came back. Alone. He stepped out, walked straight to Aunty Ifeyinwa, and said, "My mother wants to see the bride's family. All of them. Including… her." And he pointed at Ugochi.

Ugochi swallowed so hard everybody heard it.

But do you know the funny thing about this whole situation?

Remember what Aunty Ifeyinwa explained on Thursday evening while Ugochi was outside buying suya? Mama Kelechi's insults were never real. It was the family's old custom. The test. She had insulted the compound, the jollof and the bride on purpose, just to see if the bride's people could keep calm. Nneka had kept calm. Aunty had kept calm. Even the drinks boy had kept calm. Only one person in the entire compound had failed the test. And she had failed it with a broom.

That evening, the whole family went to Kelechi's house. Mama Kelechi sat in her big chair and looked at Ugochi for a very long time. Then, to everybody's surprise, she burst out laughing. "Forty years," she said, wiping her eyes. "Forty years I have been doing this test. Nobody has ever chased my brothers with a broom." Then her face became serious again. "The bride passed. The marriage can continue. But on one condition. That one…" She pointed at Ugochi. "She will not be the chief bridesmaid."

Ugochi opened her mouth to argue. Aunty Ifeyinwa stepped on her foot. Nneka squeezed her hand. And the goat, which Kelechi's uncles had finally caught and brought back, bleated loudly from the corner as if to say, "Please, don't start again." So Ugochi closed her mouth and nodded.

The next morning, Ugochi went to see Nneka at the shop. And you might think she came to apologise. But no. Instead, she started complaining. Why didn't anybody tell her about the test? Why would a grown woman insult people on purpose? And who sells suya at the exact moment of an important meeting?

Nneka, who had said sorry to the whole of Umuoji for fifteen years, finally put down her broom.

"Ugochi," she said quietly, "if a mosquito landed on my arm, you would burn down the whole house to kill it."

For the first time in her life, Ugochi had no reply.

And believe me, deep down, she knew Nneka was right. Because Ugochi had not always been like this. She grew up as the only girl among five brothers. In that house, if you did not shout, nobody heard you. If you did not fight, nobody defended you. And when she was small, the older girls at school used to bully her, and nobody ever stood up for her. Not once. So the day she met quiet little Nneka in primary school, she made herself a promise. Nobody will ever touch this one. Not while I am alive.

She kept that promise. She just never stopped to ask if Nneka needed it.

That afternoon, Ugochi did something nobody in Umuoji had ever seen. She went to the tomato seller and said sorry. Then she went to the choir mistress and said sorry. Then she went to the customer she had chased out of Nneka's shop, and said sorry to her, her sister, her neighbour, and her neighbour's sister. By evening, she had apologised so many times that the children in the compound started calling her Sister Sorry Number Two.

From that day, Ugochi made a new rule for herself. Before fighting anybody on Nneka's behalf, she would first ask one question. "Nneka, do you want me to fight?" And nine times out of ten, Nneka would answer, "No, Ugochi. Please. Just smile."

The wedding was beautiful. Ugochi was not the chief bridesmaid. She was given a new title, specially created by Mama Kelechi herself. Head of Security. She stood at the gate all day in a beautiful new gown, smiling sweetly at every guest, and not one single fight happened. Well, almost. When the DJ played the wrong song for the bride's entrance, Ugochi turned slowly, looked at Nneka, and raised one eyebrow. Nneka shook her head. And Ugochi, with great effort, sat back down.

But Ugochi did not change completely. Up till today, if you step on Nneka's foot at the market, Ugochi will still step on yours. Only now, she will first turn to Nneka and ask for permission.

And if trouble ever needed a best friend, it would still choose Ugochi. The only difference is that now, before she starts a fight with trouble, she would first ask Nneka.
