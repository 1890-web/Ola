If complaining were an Olympic sport, Ogbonna would win the gold medal. And then he would complain that the medal was too heavy.

If you served him soup, it was too salty. If you added less salt, it was too tasteless. If it rained, the rain was too wet. And if the sun came out, Ogbonna would look up at the sky and say, "See this sun, too proud. Who does it think it is?"

Whenever anybody asked why he complained so much, Ogbonna would adjust his cap and say, "My brother, I am not complaining. I am just telling the truth. Is it my fault the truth is always bad?"

In the village of Umuoji, everybody knew Ogbonna. In church, the sermon was too long. At the market, the tomatoes were too small and the traders were too proud. At the barber's shop, the haircut was too short, even when he had asked for it to be short. Even at weddings, the jollof rice was too red, the music was too loud, and the couple were smiling too much.

The village had long given up trying to please him. Mama Ijeoma, who sold akara by the roadside, had started giving him his akara before he could even open his mouth, with a small extra piece she called the complaint package. Emeka the barber had added a new price list in his head. Haircut, one hundred naira. Haircut for Ogbonna, one hundred and fifty, because of the noise.

But here is the strange part. Ogbonna was a very good carpenter. No, not just good. Excellent. His chairs were strong, his doors were straight, and his tables could carry a whole family's Christmas rice without shaking. People came from neighbouring villages to buy his furniture. They just made sure to collect it quickly, before he could start complaining about their payment.

There was only one thing Ogbonna's furniture could not get him. A wife.

Every woman he had ever courted ran away within three weeks. One left after he complained that her laugh was too loud. Another left after he complained that she walked too slowly. The third one didn't even give a reason. She simply packed her bag and sent a little boy to deliver the message: "Aunty said she has travelled."

His mother was tired. Every Sunday after church, she would hold her chest and say, "Ogbonna, I want to carry my grandchildren before I die. Is it by force to complain?"

And so, one afternoon, Mama Ogbonna took matters into her own hands. She travelled to the next village, Umuawa, and came back with news. There was a young woman called Chinasa. Calm, beautiful, hardworking, and according to everybody, the most agreeable woman in the whole of Umuawa.

The first time Ogbonna visited, he sat down and immediately said, "This chair is not comfortable."

Chinasa smiled sweetly. "You are right. It is not comfortable at all."

Ogbonna blinked. He waited for the argument. It never came. So he tried again. "And this weather is too hot."

"Very hot," Chinasa agreed. "The sun is too proud."

For the first time in his life, Ogbonna had found somebody who agreed with him. He was so happy he forgot to complain for almost ten minutes. By the time he left, he had already decided. This was the woman he would marry.

On his second visit, he complained that the road to Umuawa was too dusty. Chinasa agreed. On his third visit, he complained that the palm wine was too sweet. Chinasa agreed again. On his fourth visit, he complained that her goat was looking at him somehow. And guess what? Chinasa agreed that the goat had been looking at him somehow. Ogbonna went home that evening whistling. He told everyone who would listen that God had finally answered his prayers.

On the day of the bride price, Chinasa's mother pulled him aside. She was a small, smiling woman with very sharp eyes. "My son," she said quietly, "my daughter will agree with everything you say. So please, be careful what you say."

But Ogbonna only heard the first part. Agree with everything? Perfect. He laughed, thanked her, and went back to his palm wine.

Remember that warning. It is important.

The wedding was beautiful. Ogbonna complained about the canopy, the drummers, and the size of the meat, of course. But every time he complained, Chinasa nodded beside him and said, "You are right, my husband." And for the first few days of their marriage, Ogbonna felt like a king.

Then came the soup.

On their first Sunday together, Chinasa made egusi soup. Ogbonna tasted it, frowned, and said, "This soup is too salty."

"You are right, my husband," Chinasa said. "Too salty."

The next day, there was no salt in the soup. None at all. Ogbonna tasted it and his face scattered. "Chinasa, this soup has no taste!"

"You are right," she said calmly. "It has no taste. And since my cooking is not good enough, I will stop cooking so I don't offend you again."

And she did.

For three days, Ogbonna ate bread and groundnut. On the fourth day, he went to Mama Ijeoma's stall and bought so much akara that she asked if there was a party in his house.

That night, Ogbonna lay on his mat staring at the ceiling, trying to understand what had happened. But he quickly convinced himself it was nothing serious. Maybe Chinasa was just tired. Maybe new wives behaved like this in Umuawa. Maybe tomorrow she would wake up and cook him a pot of soup with exactly the right amount of salt. After all, she agreed with everything he said. What could possibly go wrong with that? Or so he thought.

Hm.

But that was only the beginning.

On Wednesday, it rained. Ogbonna stood at the window and said, "This rain is too wet. It is spoiling everything."

"You are right," Chinasa said. "It is too wet." And from that moment, she refused to step outside whenever it rained. Not to fetch water, not to bring in the clothes, not even to close the gate. So Ogbonna found himself running around the compound in the rain, gathering wet clothes, while his wife watched him from the window, nodding in agreement.

On Thursday, the sun came out. Ogbonna wiped his forehead and said, "This sun is too proud."

"Very proud," Chinasa agreed. Then she shut every window and drew every curtain in the house. By afternoon, the house was so dark and hot that Ogbonna knocked his knee on his own table. The very table he made with his own hands.

That evening, he looked up at the ceiling and said, "This roof is too old. One day it will fall on our heads."

"You are right, my husband," Chinasa said. And by the next morning, two young men were already standing on top of the house with hammers, pulling off the old zinc. "My wife said the roof is too old," one of them explained, waving cheerfully from above. Ogbonna had to pay them double to nail it back before the evening rain came. And of course, the rain was too wet.

On Friday, a customer came to the workshop to collect a wardrobe. As usual, Ogbonna muttered, "These customers are always rushing me. They don't appreciate good work."

Chinasa, who was sweeping nearby, straightened up. "You are right, my husband." Then she walked straight to the customer and said, "My husband says you don't appreciate good work. Please, go and buy your wardrobe somewhere else."

The man left. With his money.

Ogbonna stood there with his mouth open like a fish that had just seen its own price at the market.

Now, you might think Ogbonna would learn his lesson at that point. But no. A man who has complained for forty years does not stop in one week. On Saturday, his mother visited, and after she left, Ogbonna sighed and said, "That woman talks too much."

The next morning, the whole village heard Chinasa greeting her mother-in-law at the market. "Good morning, Mama! My husband said you talk too much!"

Mama Ogbonna dropped her basket of pepper right there on the ground.

By the following week, Ogbonna had become afraid of his own mouth. He no longer knew what to say. He would open his mouth to complain, see Chinasa nodding, and close it again. But old habits are stubborn. And one evening, after a very long day, he finally said the words that nearly destroyed everything.

"This whole village is useless," he grumbled. "Nobody here appreciates me. I should just pack my things and leave."

Chinasa looked at him for a long moment. Then she smiled. "You are right, my husband."

The next morning, Ogbonna woke up to a strange sound. When he stepped outside, he nearly fainted. Every single thing they owned was standing in front of the house. The beds, the pots, the chairs, the wardrobe, even his carpentry tools. And Chinasa was standing on top of the table, announcing to the gathering crowd, "My husband says this village is useless and nobody appreciates him. So we are leaving today!"

The whole village came out to watch. Mama Ijeoma left her akara frying. Emeka the barber came with only half of a customer's head cut. Children climbed the mango tree to get a better view.

Ogbonna stood in the middle of it all, sweating, looking at the faces of the people he had complained about for forty years. And for the first time in his life, he had nothing to complain about. He was too ashamed.

Just when he thought the ground would open up and swallow him, his mother pushed through the crowd. She looked at the furniture, then at Chinasa, and then at her son. "Ogbonna," she said, "tell them. Tell them what you really think about this village."

Ogbonna swallowed. He looked at Mama Ijeoma, who gave him extra akara every morning. He looked at Emeka, who had cut his hair for twenty years without complaining back. He looked at the old men, the children, the neighbours who had bought his chairs and tables. Then, very slowly, he said, "This village... is a good village."

The crowd went silent. Somebody gasped. Mama Ijeoma had to sit down.

"Is that so?" Chinasa asked.

"Yes," Ogbonna said. "The people are good. The akara is good. Even the sun… is not too proud."

Chinasa smiled, climbed down from the table, and said, "You are right, my husband. Let's carry everything back inside."

And that was how the whole village of Umuoji, who had spent years avoiding Ogbonna, spent one long Saturday morning helping him carry his beds, pots and wardrobe back into his house. Mama Ijeoma carried the pots. Emeka carried the chairs. Even the children carried the spoons. And not once, not even one single time, did Ogbonna complain that they were carrying anything the wrong way.

But do you know the funny thing about this whole situation? Remember what Chinasa's mother told Ogbonna at the bride price? "My daughter will agree with everything you say. So please, be careful what you say." Chinasa was not a woman without opinions. Far from it. Her mother had raised her with one special skill. When a person complains too much, don't argue with them. Agree with them, and let them hear how they sound. And Mama Ogbonna had known all along. That was exactly why she went all the way to Umuawa.

That evening, Ogbonna went to the barber's shop. And you might think he went to say sorry. But no. Instead, he started complaining about his wife. She agreed too much. She took everything too seriously. Why couldn't she just argue like a normal woman?

Emeka put down his clippers and looked at him. "Ogbonna, if God himself sent you an angel from heaven, you would complain that her wings were blocking the door."

And believe me, deep down, Ogbonna knew Emeka was right. Because complaining had not started as a bad habit. Ogbonna's father had been a master carpenter who never praised anything. If a young Ogbonna made a stool, his father would only point at the one crooked leg. So Ogbonna grew up believing that finding fault was what made a man look clever. Nobody had ever told him that it also made a man lonely.

From that day, things began to change in Ogbonna's house. He made a new rule for himself. Before every complaint, he had to say one good thing first. "The soup is very sweet, my wife… maybe just a little less salt." "The customers pay well… even if they rush me small." Chinasa started cooking again, the windows stayed open, and the customers came back.

But Ogbonna did not change completely. Up till today, whenever it rains in Umuoji, he still stands at his window and mutters that the rain is too wet. Only now, he says it very quietly, so his wife won't hear and agree.

And if complaining were still an Olympic sport, Ogbonna would still win the gold medal. Only now, before complaining about the weight, he would first thank the judges.
