If gossip were a newspaper, Mama Nkechi would be the editor, the reporter and the vendor. And she would still deliver the news before it even happened.

If your goat was pregnant, Mama Nkechi knew before the goat. If your husband came home late on Friday, the whole market heard about it by Saturday morning. And if you sneezed twice in your parlour, by evening Mama Nkechi would be telling people that you had malaria, typhoid, and possibly a secret debt.

Whenever anybody told her to mind her business, Mama Nkechi would adjust her headwrap, lean forward, and say, "My sister, I am not gossiping. I am informing. Is it my fault that I see things before other people?"

In the village of Umuoji, Mama Nkechi sold crayfish and ogbono at the market, but everybody knew her real business was news. Her stall was the busiest one in the whole market, and not because of the crayfish. Every morning, women gathered there for what they secretly called the Amebo Bulletin. Who borrowed money. Who quarrelled with her mother-in-law. Whose son failed his exams. Whose daughter came back from Lagos with a new phone and no explanation.

The village had long learned to adjust. Women in Umuoji only whispered their secrets when it was raining, because the sound of the rain covered their voices. The pastor shortened his announcements in church, because Mama Nkechi had usually announced everything the day before. And the old town crier, who used to beat his gong to share the news of the village, had become so jobless that he started selling groundnuts. "Why should I beat gong?" he complained. "By the time I reach the junction, Mama Nkechi has already finished."

But here is the strange part. Mama Nkechi was not a bad woman. No, no, no. She was the first to visit when someone was sick. She cooked the best pepper soup at every burial. And one year, when a little boy got lost on market day, it was Mama Nkechi who found him in less than one hour, because she knew which child belonged to which family, and which family was visiting which relative.

So people tolerated her. They just made sure to keep their secrets far away from her stall.

Mama Nkechi lived with her husband, Papa Nkechi, a quiet man who spent most of his evenings reading old newspapers on the veranda. Their only daughter, Nkechi, was a nurse in Enugu. She was calm, hardworking, and she called her mother every Sunday evening. Mama Nkechi was very proud of her. "My Nkechi tells me everything," she liked to boast. "Unlike some people's daughters."

One Monday morning, Mama Nkechi saw something that made her drop her crayfish scoop.

A young woman, wearing big dark sunglasses and a wrapper tied over her head like a disguise, was standing at the fabric section of the market. She was buying lace. Expensive lace. Bridal lace. And she was buying it very quietly, the way people buy things they do not want anybody to know about.

Mama Nkechi narrowed her eyes. Her nose began to itch. And when Mama Nkechi's nose itched, it meant only one thing. A story was coming.

By Tuesday, she had more clues. The caterer, Mama Ebere, had received an order for three hundred plates of jollof rice for Saturday, and refused to say who ordered it. The tailor was sewing a beautiful bridal gown and smiled mysteriously whenever anybody asked whose it was. And on Wednesday, Mama Nkechi saw a white car with ribbons parked behind the village hall, covered with a tarpaulin.

That was enough. By Wednesday afternoon, the Amebo Bulletin had its biggest story of the year.

"Somebody is having a secret wedding in this village," Mama Nkechi announced to the women at her stall, lowering her voice so that only twenty people could hear. "Three hundred plates of jollof. Bridal lace. A car with ribbons. And nobody has been invited. You know what that means? The family is ashamed. The girl ran away from her mother's house. The groom has no work. And they are hiding everything because they don't want people to talk."

The women gasped. Mama Nkechi smiled. There was nothing in this world she loved more than the sound of a gasp.

But Mama Ifeoma, who sold vegetables at the next stall, shook her head. "Mama Nkechi, wait. Are you sure about all this? You didn't even see the bride's face. How do you know she ran away from her mother?"

Mama Nkechi raised one hand like a lawyer in court.

"Even though, even though," she said. "Even though I did not see her face, I saw her wrapper. And a wrapper tied like that over the head? That is the wrapper of someone running away from her mother. My eyes have never lied to me."

Mama Ifeoma just sighed and went back to her tomatoes.

Remember that wrapper. It is important.

That evening, Mama Nkechi went home bursting with the story. She told it to Papa Nkechi on the veranda, adding a few new details she had remembered on the way. The jollof was now five hundred plates. The car was now three cars. And the groom was now a man who owed money in two villages.

Papa Nkechi lowered his newspaper slowly. "Mama Nkechi," he said, "before you talk about other people's children like this, remember that you also have a daughter."

Mama Nkechi laughed. "Even though, even though! My Nkechi is in Enugu, working at the hospital. My daughter tells me everything. If she wanted to marry, I would be the first to know."

Papa Nkechi looked at her for a long moment. Then he lifted his newspaper again and said nothing at all.

By Thursday, the story had travelled to every corner of Umuoji. Mama Nkechi told it at the water well. She told it at the women's meeting. She even told it at the clinic, to a man who had only come to treat his toothache. And with every telling, the story grew. By Friday, the bride had run away from home twice, the groom had three wives in Lagos, and the jollof had become one thousand plates.

But something strange was happening.

For the first time in her life, people were not gathering around her stall. When she walked towards a group of women, they suddenly stopped talking and started discussing the price of onions. Her own husband kept whispering on the phone and hanging up whenever she entered the room. And on Friday evening, when Nkechi called from Enugu, the call lasted only one minute. "Mama, I'm very busy. I will talk to you later."

Mama Nkechi frowned. Then she waved it away. "They are all jealous," she told herself. "Jealous that I always know first. Tomorrow, I will go and see this secret wedding with my own eyes, and then everybody will know that Mama Nkechi is never wrong."

Or so she thought.

On Saturday morning, Papa Nkechi announced that he was travelling to a cousin's house and would be back in the evening. Mama Nkechi was delighted. Nobody to stop her investigation.

She tied a dark scarf over her head. She borrowed her husband's sunglasses. She even wore her oldest wrapper, so that nobody would recognise her. Then she followed the sound of drumming all the way to the village hall.

Hm. That was where the real trouble began.

The hall was beautifully decorated. Gold and white fabric hung from the ceiling. Flowers stood on every table. And there were not three hundred guests. There were not one thousand guests. It looked like the whole of Umuoji was there. Mama Ifeoma was there. The pastor was there. Even the old town crier was there, selling groundnuts at the entrance.

"So everybody was invited except me?" Mama Nkechi whispered angrily.

She crept along the back wall and hid behind a big canopy pole. She could not see the high table properly, so she pulled a plastic chair, climbed on top of it, and stretched her neck like a giraffe.

And then, because Mama Nkechi had never been able to keep a story inside her mouth, she announced loudly to the guests near her, "You see? This is the secret wedding I told you about! The girl ran away from her mother! The poor woman doesn't even know her daughter is getting married today! What kind of shameless family—"

The music stopped.

The MC lifted his microphone. "Ladies and gentlemen, please rise for the bride!"

The doors opened. The bride walked in, wearing the beautiful lace from the market. Her face was glowing. Her smile was shy. And when she lifted her veil, Mama Nkechi saw her face clearly for the first time.

It was Nkechi.

Her Nkechi. Her only daughter.

Mama Nkechi's sunglasses slid down her nose. Her mouth fell open. And the plastic chair beneath her, which had been carrying more news than it was built for, finally gave up. Mama Nkechi fell backwards, straight into a big cooler of jollof rice.

The whole hall turned around.

The MC, who had clearly been waiting for this moment, smiled into his microphone. "And now, ladies and gentlemen, let us welcome the mother of the bride! Mama Nkechi, please, come forward!"

Five hundred faces watched as Mama Nkechi climbed out of the cooler, with jollof rice on her scarf, her husband's sunglasses hanging from one ear, and a fried chicken wing stuck to her wrapper.

But do you know the funny thing about this whole situation?

Remember the young woman at the market, with the wrapper tied over her head? That was Nkechi. She had come home secretly to buy her own wedding lace. The three hundred plates of jollof were ordered by Papa Nkechi himself. And the wedding was not hidden because the family was ashamed. It was hidden for one reason only. Nkechi wanted to surprise her mother on her birthday, which was that very Saturday. And she knew that if she told her mother on Monday, the whole of Umuoji would know by Monday afternoon, and the surprise would be finished before it even started.

So the entire village had kept one secret from the Amebo of Umuoji. For one full week. Every single person. That was why the women stopped talking when she came near. That was why the phone calls were short. And that was why Papa Nkechi had warned her, very clearly, to remember that she also had a daughter.

After the ceremony, Mama Nkechi sat down beside her husband. And you might think she would finally admit that she had been wrong. But no.

"Even though, even though," she began. "Even though I made a small mistake, why would you people hide my own daughter's wedding from me? Is that how a family behaves?"

Mama Ifeoma, who was sitting nearby with a plate of jollof, put down her spoon.

"Mama Nkechi," she said, "if your own shadow had a secret, you would announce it before the sun came out."

The whole table laughed. Even Papa Nkechi laughed. And for once, Mama Nkechi had nothing to say.

And believe me, deep down, she knew Mama Ifeoma was right. Because Mama Nkechi had not always been like this. Many years ago, when she first married into Umuoji, she was a stranger. Nobody visited her. Nobody greeted her at the market. She spent her days alone at her stall, watching other women laugh together. Then one day, she shared a small piece of news she had overheard, and suddenly, three women gathered around her stall to listen. From that day, she learned that news brought people close. And slowly, without noticing, she began to care more about the story than about the people inside it.

That night, Nkechi hugged her mother. "Mama, I wanted to tell you myself. I wanted you to hear it from my mouth, not from the market."

Mama Nkechi held her daughter tightly and cried, partly from joy, and partly because she had spent a whole week telling the entire village that her own daughter was a runaway bride.

From that day, things began to change. The very next morning, Mama Nkechi went round the whole of Umuoji correcting her own story. She went to the well, the clinic, the church, and the women's meeting. And she made herself a new rule. Before sharing any news, she would ask herself two questions. Did I see it with my own eyes? And is it mine to tell?

The women of Umuoji even gave her a special award at the end of the year. Amebo of the Year. But this time, it was for spreading only good news. New babies. New jobs. New houses. And of course, new weddings.

But Mama Nkechi did not change completely. Up till today, if your goat is pregnant, she will still know before the goat. Only now, she will come to your house first and ask, "Can I tell people?"

And if gossip were still a newspaper, Mama Nkechi would still be the editor. The only difference is that now, before she prints any story, she checks the facts first. Even though, even though.
