If greed ever needed a place to stay, it would rent a room from Papa Ebuka. And by the end of the month, Papa Ebuka would increase its rent.

If you moved into his compound in January, your rent went up in April. If the rain fell in June, there was a rain fee. If the harmattan came in December, there was a dust fee. And if you dared to ask him why, Papa Ebuka would adjust his big agbada, fold his hands behind his back, and say, "My house, my rules. If you don't like it, go and build your own."

Papa Ebuka owned the biggest compound in Umuoji. Eight rooms, one well, one mango tree, and one large wooden board nailed to the gate. That board was the most famous object in the whole village. Every Monday morning, a new rule appeared on it, written in red paint.

No visitors after seven o'clock.

No laughing loudly, it weakens the walls.

No cooking egusi on Sundays, the smell disturbs the landlord's rest.

Every bucket of water from the well is twenty naira.

Tenants must greet the landlord first. Any tenant who does not greet first will pay a respect fee.

And the latest one: Any tenant who wishes to leave must give one month's notice in writing.

Nobody knew why that last rule was there. Nobody had ever left. Not because they were happy, no, no, no. They stayed because the compound was close to the market, close to the school, and close to the main road. And because, as Teacher Okoro liked to say, "Better the devil you know than the landlord you don't."

The tenants had long learned how to survive. Teacher Okoro kept a small notebook where he calculated every new fee, just to know how much of his salary was left. Usually, it was not much. Brother Sunday the tailor had stopped singing while he sewed, because singing might count as laughing loudly. And Mama Nkem, who sold pepper soup and had lived in room four for nine years, had trained all the compound children to go quiet the moment they heard Papa Ebuka's slippers on the gravel. Flip. Flop. Silence.

You know that kind of silence in a classroom when the headmaster walks past the window? Yes. That was the kind of silence that followed Papa Ebuka everywhere he went. Even the goat that slept under the mango tree had learned to stand up when he passed.

But here is the funny part. Papa Ebuka was not poor. Far from it. He had a car, two plots of land, and a new storey building half-finished in the next village. But according to him, money was like water in a basket. If you don't keep pouring, it will finish.

There was only one person in the compound who could still talk to him without paying a fee. His wife, Mama Ebuka. She was a gentle woman who often slipped a plate of rice to a tenant whose rent was late, and quietly paid for medicine when a child was sick. Without her, the tenants said, the compound would have emptied years ago.

One afternoon, Mama Ebuka came back from the market with news. "Papa Ebuka," she said, "Chief Okafor has finished building rooms at the junction. Ten rooms. They say the rent is fair, the water is free, and there is no board on his gate."

Papa Ebuka laughed so hard his cap shifted. "Free water? Let him enjoy his poverty. Who will leave my compound? Who will leave the best location in Umuoji?"

"I am only saying," Mama Ebuka said softly. "People are talking. Be careful."

But Papa Ebuka had already stopped listening. In his head, he was busy calculating the Christmas fee.

That Sunday evening, the tenants held a quiet meeting under the mango tree. Papa Ebuka saw them from his window and smiled. "Let them gather," he said to himself. "They are probably planning how to pay the Christmas fee early." But if he had opened the window, he would have heard Mama Nkem say, very clearly, "One more rule. Just one more rule, and we will all leave on the same day." And he would have seen every single tenant nod.

That same week, small envelopes started appearing under his door. One from Teacher Okoro. One from Brother Sunday. One from the young couple in room six. One from Mama Nkem. Papa Ebuka picked them up, looked at the handwriting, and without opening a single one, dropped them all into a basket in his parlour. A basket with one word written on it. Complaints.

Because, according to the board, the landlord does not read complaints.

Remember that basket. It is important.

On the first day of December, Papa Ebuka woke up early, as he always did on rent day. He took his bath slowly. He wore his finest blue agbada, the one with gold embroidery that made him look like a chief. He brushed his cap, polished his shoes, and picked up his big black rent book. Then he walked to the gate, pulled out his red paint, and added a brand new rule to the board.

Christmas season fee: five thousand naira per room. Payable today.

He stepped back and admired his handwriting. "Beautiful," he said to himself. "Simply beautiful."

But as he turned around to begin his rounds, something felt strange.

The compound was quiet. Not the usual quiet, when the children saw him coming. A different kind of quiet. Brother Sunday's sewing machine was not rattling. Mama Nkem's pepper soup pot was not on the fire. Even the clothesline, which was always heavy with wrappers and school uniforms, was completely empty. And when Teacher Okoro walked past the gate with a big carton on his head, he did not greet first. He did not greet at all. Papa Ebuka quickly opened his rent book and wrote, Teacher Okoro, respect fee, two hundred naira. Hm. He would collect it later, together with the Christmas fee.

Papa Ebuka frowned. Then he waved it away. "It is market day," he told himself. "They have all gone to buy things for Christmas. Good. They will come back with money."

Or so he thought.

At exactly ten o'clock, a big lorry stopped in front of the gate. Papa Ebuka watched in confusion as the driver jumped down and opened the back. Then the doors of the rooms began to open, one after another.

Teacher Okoro came out carrying a table. Brother Sunday came out carrying his sewing machine on his head. The young couple from room six came out with a mattress. And Mama Nkem came out carrying her big pepper soup pot, with all the compound children marching behind her like a small army, each one holding a pillow, a stool or a bucket.

Papa Ebuka's rent book fell from his hand.

"What is this? What is happening here?"

Nobody answered him. They just kept carrying.

So Papa Ebuka did something nobody in Umuoji had ever seen him do. He ran. In his heavy agbada, in his polished shoes, he ran from room to room.

"Teacher! Teacher Okoro! Ah, my good teacher! The Christmas fee, forget it! It was a joke!"

Teacher Okoro adjusted his glasses and carried his table to the lorry.

"Brother Sunday! The water, no more twenty naira! Ten naira! Okay, five!"

Brother Sunday loaded his sewing machine without even looking back.

Then Papa Ebuka did the unthinkable. He rushed to the gate, grabbed the famous board with both hands, and started tearing off the rules one by one. "No more visitors' rule! Laugh as loud as you like! Cook egusi every day if you want! Even on Sunday!"

The tenants stopped for a moment and watched him. Then, very politely, they continued packing.

So Papa Ebuka changed strategy. He ran to the back of the lorry and sat down right on top of Brother Sunday's mattress. "If you are leaving," he announced, "you will carry me along. Since I am the landlord, wherever you go is my compound." The children laughed so loudly that, according to the old rule, the walls should have fallen down. Teacher Okoro sighed, and with great respect, he and Brother Sunday lifted Papa Ebuka, agbada and all, and set him down gently in the middle of the yard like a bag of rice.

By noon, the lorry was full. Mama Nkem was the last to climb in. Papa Ebuka ran after the lorry, holding up his cap, shouting, "Mama Nkem! Nine years! Nine years we have been together!" But the lorry turned at the junction and disappeared towards Chief Okafor's new rooms, leaving nothing behind but a cloud of red dust.

Papa Ebuka stood alone in the middle of his compound.

Eight empty rooms. One quiet well. One mango tree. And one torn board, hanging from a single nail.

He sat down on the steps with his rent book in his lap. And slowly, very slowly, he began to understand something no school had ever taught him. An empty room does not pay rent.

And guess what? The bills did not care.

That same evening, the man from the electricity office arrived with a bill. The next morning, the bricklayers from his half-finished building in the next village came for their money. Then came Ebuka's school fees, the church Christmas levy, and the bag of rice he had promised his in-laws. For the first time in his life, Papa Ebuka opened his rent book and found nothing to collect. Not even from the goat that sometimes slept under the mango tree. The goat looked at him, chewed slowly, and walked out through the gate. Even the goat had found somewhere cheaper.

Just when he thought things could not get worse, Mama Ebuka walked into the parlour. She did not shout. She did not say, "I told you." She simply went to the basket in the corner, the one with the word complaints written on it, and turned it upside down on the table.

The envelopes spilled out.

"Open them," she said.

But do you know the funny thing about this whole situation?

Those envelopes were not complaints at all. Every single one of them was a letter of notice. Neatly written, properly dated, exactly one month before. Teacher Okoro had even signed his with a stamp. The tenants had followed Papa Ebuka's own rule. Any tenant who wishes to leave must give one month's notice in writing. They had given it. He had simply thrown it into the basket without reading.

The next morning, Papa Ebuka went to Mama Nkem at Chief Okafor's compound. And you might think he went there to apologise. But no. Instead, he started complaining. Chief Okafor was stealing his tenants. The tenants were ungrateful. And who leaves a landlord in December, of all months?

Mama Nkem put down her pepper soup ladle and looked at him for a long time.

"Papa Ebuka," she said, "if we paid you rent for the air in that compound, you would charge us extra for breathing out."

Some of the people nearby laughed. Papa Ebuka did not. Because deep down, he knew she was right.

And believe me, Papa Ebuka had not always been like this. Many years ago, when he was a young apprentice with nothing, he had rented a tiny room from a landlord who threw his box into the rain because he paid three days late. That night, sleeping on a bench at the motor park, young Ebuka made himself a promise. One day, I will be the landlord. And I will never be on the losing side again.

He kept that promise. He just never noticed that he had become the very man he was running from.

That evening, Papa Ebuka did something new. He went to Chief Okafor's compound, knocked on each door, and said the one word nobody had ever heard from his mouth. Sorry.

He did not stop there. He sat with Mama Ebuka and Teacher Okoro, and they worked out a fair rent. One price for the whole year. No season fees. Water from the well for free. And the big board on the gate was taken down and burnt behind the mango tree.

It took some weeks. But one by one, the tenants came back. Brother Sunday was the first, because his customers knew where to find him. Mama Nkem came last, and only after Papa Ebuka bought two bowls of her pepper soup every evening for a whole week.

On the day the last tenant moved back in, Mama Nkem cooked egusi on a Sunday. The smell filled the whole compound. The children laughed as loudly as they wanted. And Papa Ebuka, who used to make a new rule every single week, sat under the mango tree with a plate of egusi in his hand and did not write a single new rule that week.

But Papa Ebuka did not change completely. Up till today, there is still one small board on his gate, written in red paint. It has only one rule. No tenant is allowed to laugh louder than the landlord.

And if greed ever needed a place to stay, it would still try to rent a room from Papa Ebuka. The only difference is that now, it would get a fair price, free water, and a receipt.
